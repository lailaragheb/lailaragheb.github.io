The challenge

As a digital bank prepared for launch, content practices lacked the shared foundations needed to scale consistently across journeys and teams.

Content varied across customer journeys, ownership and review processes weren't clearly defined, and writers lacked a centralized source of truth for writing standards and brand tonality. Without documented guidelines and repeatable workflows, maintaining consistency depended heavily on individual knowledge, making it harder to collaborate effectively and onboard new writers.

The challenge was to establish a content design system that would bring these practices together, create clarity around how content was produced and reviewed, and give the team a foundation for scaling content operations.

The objective

Create a centralized content design system that would:

  • Establish consistent English and Arabic content standards across customer journeys.
  • Define content ownership, responsibilities, review stages, and approval workflows.
  • Provide a single source of truth for brand voice, tone, terminology, and editorial guidelines.
  • Standardize component-level writing rules to support consistent UI content.
  • Make content practices easier to understand, maintain, and teach to new team members.

The solution

I built the system around four connected pieces, so writers could find answers to three questions in one place: how we sound, how content moves from idea to release, and how each UI component is written.

1. Content foundations and Arabic tonality

Shared voice, tone, editorial, terminology, and localization standards for consistent English and Arabic content.

Arabic tonality was the hardest decision in the system. Egyptian banking Arabic has no single standard, so I benchmarked how leading Egyptian banks and fintechs write across five dimensions: formality, dialect, simplicity, warmth, and actionability. Traditional banks leaned on formal, information-first Modern Standard Arabic with heavy financial terminology and inconsistent CTAs. Fintechs used shorter, warmer Egyptian Arabic. Banks tended to speak from a position of authority, fintechs from a position of partnership.

To help leadership decide, I wrote the same content in three registers (Modern Standard, moderate Egyptian, and full Egyptian Arabic) and mocked them up on the homepage, marketplace, product catalog, and rewards screens. The CEO and stakeholders chose moderate Egyptian Arabic, a voice that sounds like a partner rather than an institution. The full benchmark and the three-register test are in Choosing an Arabic Voice for a Digital Bank.

2. Establishing a repeatable content lifecycle

I mapped the content workflow into four phases, defining the triggers, responsibilities, actions, and outputs for each stage. The process connected product planning, UX writing, design, development, testing, and stakeholder sign-off, giving teams a shared reference for how content moves from requirements to release. One decision I pushed for: once content has been reviewed and published, any later change must be documented and sent as a change request email to all stakeholders, with the CDO's approval. This keeps published content from being edited informally after sign-off.

3. Establishing content governance and operations

I defined content ownership, review stages, handoffs, and approval requirements at each phase of the lifecycle.

4. Turning content principles into actionable component rules

I documented writing rules at the component level, including CTA construction, character limits, and examples. This helped translate high-level content principles into practical guidance that teams could apply during design and content reviews.

The outcome

Once the system was ready, I presented it and onboarded the entire design team, including the other UX writers. It is now the handover and onboarding material for new UX writers, and it teaches the content workflow and lifecycle. We are also sharing it with marketing and commercial teams, so their content follows the same guidelines as the product.

What I can and can't say about impact. The app hasn't launched, so there are no customer metrics yet, and I haven't measured review rounds or onboarding time. The signal so far is qualitative. Writers have one place to check a rule instead of relying on individual knowledge, which means fewer human errors while writing, and new hires start from the documented workflow instead of from whoever happens to be nearby.
