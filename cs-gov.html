The challenge

The app is a trusted one-stop shop for government services and bills, and it wants residents to open it every day, not only when they need a service. That means a more human voice, without losing the trust that makes a government app work.

Questions before writing

I started with eleven questions, from who the audience is and what the current tone is, to how a parent adds a child in the school-bus feature. Where I couldn't get answers, I wrote down my assumptions:

  • The app's voice today is formal, transactional, and information-first, with Modern Standard Arabic.
  • Its copy leans on long sentences and generic CTAs that don't say what the action is.
  • Its microcopy is inconsistent. For example, headers alternate between two Arabic verb forms.

Benchmarking

I compared Careem, Talabat, Airbnb, Experience Abu Dhabi, and DubaiNow against the app today. The lessons:

  • Organise content around people's needs, not government entities.
  • Use context and moments to make content feel relevant, not administrative.
  • Add discovery alongside utility.
  • Simplify government language without losing authority.

The strategy

The opportunity is to shift from administrative completion to proactive guidance.

The opportunity isn't to make the app "more casual." It's to make it more human, relevant and proactive while preserving the trust and clarity expected from a government platform.

That became six tonality principles: Human, Helpful, Proactive, Relevant, Discoverable, and Trustworthy.

Putting it to work

Confirmation email. The old email led with an official statement and ended with a generic sentence, and its link didn't say where it went. I made the subject line the main message ("Your New Lease Contract Registration Is Under Review") and restructured the body as what happened, what you need to do, what happens next, and how we can help. I also applied one rule throughout: always use contractions.

Push notification. Limited space means one job: say what's due and what to do. I removed the ID number and the help instructions, used "Due in 3 days" instead of a date, kept the brand name in English, and used English numerals in Arabic. The result: "Your utility bill is due in 3 days. Pay it with a few taps on [App]." It's about a third shorter than the original and readable at a glance.

Complaint form. The old form read like a government form. "Add more details" and "Tell us more about it" implied something had already been entered, "Click" doesn't fit a mobile app, and "Preferred response channel" is robotic. I replaced them with "Tell us what happened so we can understand the issue" and "How would you like to hear from us?", explained why the optional attachments help, and ended with "By tapping 'Submit complaint'…".

School-bus widget. Every widget state answers one question: what does the parent need to know right now? "Studies now!" became "Mohammad is at school now. Bus leaves at 2:00 PM", and "rests now" became "No active journey. Next trip on 12 Mar." I led with the benefit, and avoided possessive adjectives in CTAs, using "Add a child".

School-bus onboarding. My first three screens said nearly the same thing, so I rebuilt the story around four feelings: reassurance (I know my child is on their journey), convenience (I know when to expect them), control (I know when something changes), and action (I can access it all from the app).

The style guide

Editorial standards for capitalization, numbers and currency, dates and time, CTAs, and English numerals in Arabic copy, plus the six pillars, each with a before-and-after example. For instance, "Your application has been submitted successfully" becomes "We've received your application."
