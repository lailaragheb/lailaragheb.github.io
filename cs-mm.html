The challenge

MoneyMore reads a user's bank SMS messages to log spending automatically, then helps them stay within their budgets. Writing for it meant solving three problems at once:

  • Trust. Asking someone to let an app read their SMS can feel invasive, so the copy has to explain why.
  • Feeling judged. Budgeting is hard, and apps that scold users for overspending lose them.
  • Two languages, several registers. Egyptian users expect Arabic that sounds like them. Formal Arabic feels like a bank, and heavy slang feels unserious.

What I found

I benchmarked local disruptors (Telda and Lucky), regional leaders (Thndr and Sarwa), and global SMS-tracking tools. The global tools were functional and dry, and their Arabic often felt translated, with phrasing no Egyptian would say. They read like a calculator, not a companion.

The opportunity was a voice as clear and educational as Thndr's, as local as Telda's without the slang, and honest about how the technology works.

The voice

I defined four tonality dimensions, each with a do and a don't.

  • Empowering, not judging. "Looks like you went a bit over on Dining this week. No worries, we can adjust next week's goal." Never "You spent too much."
  • Vigilant. Because the app reads SMS, it stays on the pulse and catches things so the user doesn't have to.
  • Local, but not slang. Everyday Egyptian Arabic ("white Ammiya"), not the formal phrasing of a notice.
  • Transparent. Explain the "why" behind the technology: "We only look at your bank SMS to save you the hassle of typing. Your personal chats are 100% private."

One rule for two registers

To satisfy a bilingual Egyptian audience, I proposed an "Official vs. Social" rule, then reviewed it with the marketing director and the team until we agreed on the decisions.

  • Official content is written in Modern Standard Arabic. That covers privacy policies, terms, bank balance totals, and security alerts. It should feel professional, secure, and bank-like: "رصيدك الإجمالي في جميع البنوك".
  • Social content is written in white Ammiya. That covers push notifications, onboarding, category names, and tips. It should feel friendly and street-smart: "خروجة النهاردة كانت تقيلة شوية؟"

Reviewing the brand voice guide

The brand voice guide had a draft, and I reviewed it section by section with comments. The main changes:

  • Adding what was missing. I proposed the four tonality dimensions above, plus language guidelines for English and Egyptian Arabic, writing patterns, an editorial style table, a consistency checklist, and a glossary.
  • Fixing an Arabic example. One example in the Arabic section was a sentence no Egyptian would say that way. I replaced it with "المرتب نزل. تحب نقسمه قبل ما يتصرف؟" ("Your salary just landed. Want to split it before it gets spent?"), which keeps to the rule of not trying to be cool.
  • Making the hard rules realistic. I set passive voice as the expectation for error messages, so the copy never points a finger at the user, while accepting that the rule will sometimes be broken when it's unclear what went wrong. I also pushed back on "never use filler phrases": a small phrase like "Just a reminder" can soften the copy, so it depends on the context.
  • Keeping the glossary separate. It changes regularly, so I recommended keeping it apart from the guide.

The writing system

  • Guidelines. Tone, language rules, patterns for notifications, budget updates, empty states, errors and success messages, editorial style, and a consistency checklist.
  • Glossary. English terms mapped to Egyptian Arabic, such as "Expenses" to "مصروفات", "Internal transfer" to "تحويل داخلي", and "Last month" to "الشهر اللي فات". It started with the first journeys and is growing into the full glossary.
  • Details that matter in Arabic. Number-aware plurals ("عملية واحدة", then "عمليات" for 2 to 10, then "عملية" for 11 and up), character budgets for push notification titles and bodies, and screen-reader labels.

The flows

I wrote the English and Arabic copy for seven flows: homepage, budgets, transactions, offers, push notifications, profile, and subscription.

  • Homepage. A time-aware greeting, total spending for the month, a "Where it's going" breakdown by category, and latest expenses.
  • Budgets. Smart budget suggestions, budget rows with a status pill, and a quick-create sheet with validation and cap messages.
  • Transactions. Transaction details, adding, editing, and deleting with an undo, and marking internal transfers.
  • Offers. Available and claimed offers, empty and offline states, and the push notification that announces a new offer.
  • Profile. Editing a name with inline validation, adding a phone number with an OTP step, settings, and account deletion with a confirmation and a connection-lost state.
  • Push notifications. Budget alerts at 80 percent and when a budget is exceeded.

Where it stands

The app is live on Android and has 50K+ downloads on Google Play. That is the app's reach, not a result I can attribute to the copy alone. I wrote the copy for seven flows, and the work is ongoing.

Next, I'll finish the full glossary. MoneyMore is also planning to launch in the UAE and Saudi Arabia, and the Arabic content will need to be rewritten for each country. The Official vs. Social rule and the glossary should make it clearer which content is specific to Egypt and which can carry over.
