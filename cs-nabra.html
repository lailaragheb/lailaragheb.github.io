Nabra: An AI Tonality Checker for English and Arabic Content

Context
Nabra (نبرة), Arabic for "tone", an internal tool for a digital bank's content reviews
Role
Content Designer
Scope
Concept, research, prompt design, and a working prototype, in English and Arabic
Team
Solo project, later integrated into the team's central tool
Tools
Claude Code, with a Lokalise export as input
Status
Working prototype in testing
ProblemTone feedback in content reviews was opinion-based and often conflicting, with no objective way to settle it.
What I didI designed and prototyped Nabra with Claude Code. It learns a brand's voice from its own strings, then scores tonality and readability, flags avoided terms, and suggests rewrites in English and Arabic.
ResultA working prototype, tested on 50+ strings. Review rounds have reduced since the team started using it (not yet measured). My manager integrated it into the team's central tool and named it Ask Laila.

The problem

Tonality was inconsistent across the app. In content review cycles, many stakeholders gave feedback based on personal opinion, not on readability or the brand voice. Feedback often conflicted, and there was no objective way to settle it.

The idea

Build an AI tool, which I named Nabra (نبرة, Arabic for "tone"), that checks content against the brand's own voice before and during review, so conversations start from shared evidence instead of individual taste.

Before building, I worked out what someone using a review tool actually needs to see: a check on the content they wrote, a tonality score, a readability score, and better suggestions. I made suggestions the most important output, because a score alone doesn't tell a writer what to change.

How it works

Set-up, done once. The tool takes three inputs: the team's Lokalise export (an Excel file with every language), the content guidelines, and an editable list of avoided terms. Each avoided term can carry a replacement and a context note, and the list is managed separately for English and Arabic. The tool reads all the Lokalise strings in batches and builds a brand voice profile covering tone traits, English and Arabic patterns, vocabulary, and key terms. The profile is saved and reused for every review. The Arabic voice isn't hard-coded: the tool learns it from the brand's own Lokalise strings, so its suggestions follow the moderate Egyptian Arabic tonality chosen for a digital bank.

Every review. A writer enters English, Arabic, or both, and gets four outputs:

  1. Readability (English): grade level, reading ease, average sentence length, long sentences, passive voice, and adverbs.
  2. Tonality score: 0 to 100, with a label and a short explanation, based on how well the content matches the brand voice profile.
  3. Flagged terms: any avoided terms found in the content, with the reason and a replacement.
  4. Suggestions: side-by-side original and suggested rewrites, written in the same language as the content entered.

Prompt design

I briefed Claude Code to act as a software engineer building a review-cycle tool for a content team. The brief specified the three inputs, the learn-once-then-compare logic, and the four outputs. It also set one hard requirement: suggestions had to be relevant to the context and written in the language of the content entered.

The design

A two-pane editor for English and Arabic, one Analyze button, and a results bar that puts all four scores side by side.

1. Set-up. The writer uploads the team's Lokalise export (.xlsx, all languages), and the tool reports what it found: 6,365 keys, 6,364 in English and 6,157 in Arabic. The content guidelines and the avoided terms sit beside it, both editable, with separate English and Arabic term lists.

2. Readability. Grade level, reading ease, average sentence length, and counts of long sentences, passive voice, and adverbs. English only.

3. Tonality. A score out of 100 with a short explanation. In this example, "send money" and its Arabic equivalent scored 58, "Needs Work". Both read like plain system labels instead of the friendly, direct brand voice. The English is lowercase and doesn't speak to the user, and the Arabic uses formal Modern Standard phrasing instead of natural Egyptian wording.

4. Suggestions. Side-by-side rewrites with a short reason for each. "send money" becomes "Send money", and the Arabic "إرسال نقود" becomes "حوّل فلوس", with a more casual alternative, "ابعت فلوس", for sending money to a person.

What happened next

The prototype did not stay a prototype. My manager integrated it into the team's central content tool and named it Ask Laila. The Glossary, Tonality, Guidelines and Checker tabs all come from the content design system I built, and the team now uses the checker to review content before it ships to production.

The checks also run automatically when a content designer marks work as done. They cover avoided terms, tonality and readability. Meaning, English and Arabic parity, and regulated wording still need a person, and the tool says so on screen.

Where it stands

Early signal. Review rounds have reduced since the team started using the tool, though I haven't put a number on it yet. One stakeholder summed up the change:

"Now the review cycles will not be based on subjectivity anymore."

Testing. I tested the tool on more than 50 English and Arabic strings. It consistently produced sensible scores, useful rewrite suggestions, and correct avoided-term flags.
