Choosing an Arabic Voice for a Digital Bank

The problem

The bank was preparing to launch, and its Arabic had no settled voice. Egyptian banking Arabic has no single standard, from formal Modern Standard Arabic to full dialect, and the choice would shape every screen. Leadership needed evidence to decide, not opinions.

The benchmark

I assessed how leading Egyptian banks and fintechs write Arabic across five dimensions: formality, dialect, simplicity, warmth, and actionability. The set was three traditional banks, three digital fintechs, and the bank itself.

  • Traditional banks wrote mostly simplified Modern Standard Arabic. They put information first, used complex financial terms, showed little emotion, and were inconsistent on tone and on how CTAs were phrased.
  • Fintechs wrote moderate Egyptian Arabic: casual and conversational, with short sentences, a youth-oriented voice, and action-oriented CTAs.
  • The bank itself sat in the middle. It scored medium on formality and dialect, and high on simplicity, warmth, and actionability.

The market insight: traditional banks communicate from a position of authority, and fintechs from a position of partnership. Instead of "here's our product", a fintech says "here's how we can help you".

Three options, tested

I wrote the same content in three registers: Modern Standard Arabic, moderate Egyptian Arabic, and full Egyptian Arabic. I started with one message, a transfer confirmation, so the differences were easy to see:

  • Modern Standard: "تم تحويل المبلغ إلى المستفيد بنجاح. يمكنك الاطلاع على تفاصيل العملية من سجل المعاملات في أي وقت."
  • Moderate Egyptian: "تم تحويل المبلغ بنجاح للمستفيد. تقدر تشوف تفاصيل العملية من سجل المعاملات في أي وقت."
  • Full Egyptian: "حولنالك المبلغ بنجاح. تقدر تشوف كل التفاصيل في سجل المعاملات وقت ما تحب."

Then I mocked up all three on four real screens: the homepage, the marketplace, the product catalog, and the rewards screen. Side by side, stakeholders could judge each voice in context, not in the abstract.

The decision

The CEO and stakeholders chose moderate Egyptian Arabic, and it was a clear winner.

What it led to

This work became the foundation of the bank's content design system, where the same decision sits behind the Arabic standards. Read how the system was built around it. It also shapes how Nabra, the tonality checker, scores and rewrites Arabic copy.

The app hasn't launched, so there is no customer data on the voice yet. The signal I have is the decision itself: leadership chose with evidence instead of preference, and the choice now anchors the writing guidelines and the review tools.
